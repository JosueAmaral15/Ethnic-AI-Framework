1. Executive Summary

This report outlines a set of indicators and heuristics for assessing when a person can or cannot be considered trustworthy. The goal is not to create a tool for moral condemnation, public humiliation, or unfair discrimination, but rather to establish prudent criteria for granting trust, especially in contexts where a wrong decision could jeopardize someone's life, health, dignity, safety, family, assets, reputation, or emotional stability.

The central thesis of this report is:

**A trustworthy person is one who demonstrates, through repeated life patterns, a commitment to preserving their own life, the lives of others, and the healthy continuity of human society.**

In this model, trust is not a reward given for appearance, likeability, speech, social status, wealth, popularity, or promises of good intentions. Trust should be granted when there is consistent evidence of:

- responsible self-care;
- self-control;
- temperance;
- honesty;
- justice;
- prudence;
- respect for one's own dignity and that of others;
- Healthy and constructive consumption;
- Rejection of vices, corruption, exploitation, and self-destruction;
- doing good;
- useful social service;
- Responsibility regarding information, privacy, money, power, and influence.

The main formulation can be expressed as follows:

A person does not become trustworthy because they have received information, power, intimacy, money, responsibility, or influence. They must receive these things because they have first demonstrated truthfulness, care, prudence, justice, self-control, self-love, brotherly love, and preservation of life.

This inversion is crucial. The report doesn't start with the question: "How does a person act after receiving power?" The more prudent question is: "What evidence shows that this person deserves to receive some degree of power, intimacy, responsibility, or influence?"

3. Definition of trust

3.1 General Definition

**Trust** is the rational expectation that a person, upon being given access to something valuable or vulnerable, will tend to preserve, protect, respect, and develop that asset, rather than destroy, exploit, corrupt, or neglect it.

A trustworthy person is not simply someone pleasant. Nor is it just someone who speaks well, has a good appearance, possesses money, has status, or is socially accepted. A trustworthy person is one whose life demonstrates consistency between:

- what he says;
- that which is valued;
- that which consumes;
- that which one practices;
- that which encourages in others;
- what she does when she is contradicted;
- that which one does when no one is watching;
- something you do when you have the opportunity to harm someone without being discovered.

3.2 Definition oriented towards the preservation of life

In an ethics geared towards the survival of the human species, trust can be defined as follows:

**Reliability is the degree to which a person demonstrates, through physical, mental, moral, social, and professional habits, that they are oriented toward preserving their own life, the lives of others, and the healthy continuity of human society.**

On the other hand:

**Unreliability is the degree to which a person demonstrates patterns of self-destruction, destruction of others, addiction, lying, violence, uncontrolled lust, corruption, recklessness, futility, exploitation, or disregard for health and life.**

4. Principle of granting trust

Trust should be granted in proportion to the risk involved.

The greater the potential for harm, the greater the need for reliable evidence should be.

For example:

| Context | Level of trust required |
|---|---:|
Casual conversation | Low to moderate
Friendship | Moderate |
| Study partnership | Moderate |
| Common work | Moderate to high |
Romantic relationship | High |
| Wedding | Very tall |
| Childcare | Very loud |
Elderly or vulnerable care | Very high
Professional driver | Very tall |
Doctor, teacher, pastor, counselor, or therapist | Very tall |
| Politician, judge, police officer, healthcare entrepreneur, or public administrator | Extreme |

A trustworthy person is one who has demonstrated sufficient maturity to receive a certain degree of responsibility. The granting of trust should not be naive or cruel. It should be prudent, proportionate, and based on evidence.

5. The vital axis of reliability

The main hypothesis of this model is that the strongest indicators of reliability are those linked to the preservation of life.

In order of importance, the following stand out:

1. Adopting a healthier lifestyle;
2. Encouraging healthcare for others;
3. Consumption patterns consistent with health and the preservation of life;
4. self-control;
5. temperance;
6. discernment between what preserves and what destroys;
7. Rejection of vices and self-destruction;
8. Rejection of practices that exploit, harm, or degrade other people.

The reason for this is simple: physical and mental health influences almost all human capacities relevant to trust, such as:

- reasoning;
- prudence;
- self-control;
- emotional stability;
- memory;
- work capacity;
- empathy;
- patience;
- resistance to handling;
- the ability to say "no" to vices and excesses;
- ability to keep commitments;
- ability to care for other people.

This doesn't mean that every sick person is unreliable. It means that a person's pattern of relationship with their health is highly informative.

6. Health as an indicator, not as a condemnation.

It is necessary to carefully distinguish between three different situations.

6.1 Involuntary illness

A person may be ill due to factors such as:

- genetics;
- poverty;
- accident;
- labor exploitation;
- unhealthy environment;
- trauma;
- disability;
- infection;
- experience of family neglect;
- lack of access to treatment;
- unfavorable social conditions.

In these cases, illness should not be used as proof of a lack of character. A person can be ill and trustworthy, especially if they demonstrate responsibility, a desire to improve, prudence, and care for others.

6.2 Illness accompanied by seeking care

When a person recognizes their limitations, seeks treatment, tries to organize their life, accepts prudent guidance, and avoids harming others, their health condition should not be interpreted as a sign of unreliability.

On the contrary: the honest pursuit of care can be a sign of maturity.

6.3 Voluntary and repeated self-destruction

A strong warning sign emerges when there is a pattern of conscious, repeated self-destruction, indifferent to the consequences. Examples:

- Drinking to the point of frequently losing control;
- using substances that impair judgment and safety;
- driving while intoxicated;
- Sleeping irresponsibly before critical tasks;
- to feed in a destructive way without any effort to correct it;
- to disregard necessary treatment;
- to encourage others to degrade themselves;
- transforming vices into identity;
- to make self-destruction a lifestyle;
- Putting others at risk because of personal choices.

In these cases, the problem is not "being sick," but rather **deliberately disregarding life**.

Does the person want to improve?
Does the person promote health or degradation?
Does the person take responsibility for the effects of their habits?
Does the person harm others due to their negligence?

8. Consumption patterns as an indicator of trust.

Consumption reveals values. What a person consumes repeatedly helps to indicate what they normalize, admire, finance, reproduce, and encourage.

8.1 Healthy Consumption

A more reliable consumption pattern includes:

- foods that promote health;
- educational content;
Useful books;
- study practices;
- physical activity;
- services that improve life;
- Products that meet real needs;
Entertainment that does not degrade human dignity.
- prudent use of technology;
- Responsible financial consumption.

8.2 Destructive Consumption

A less reliable consumption pattern includes:

- alcoholism;
- drugs;
- degrading pornography;
- content that normalizes violence, cruelty, or humiliation;
- gambling;
- impulsive and irresponsible consumption;
Empty ostentation;
- products that cause illness;
- services that exploit vulnerable people;
- media that encourages a reversal of values;
- frivolous things that replace study, useful work, and responsibility.

8.3 Consumption and social responsibility

A trustworthy person not only avoids self-destruction, but also avoids encouraging the destruction of others.

So the question is not simply:

"What does this person consume?"

But also:

"What does this person fund, promote, normalize, and recommend?"

Anyone who encourages consumption patterns that cause illness, addiction, humiliation, sexualization, exploitation, or make people less able to live healthy and dignified lives should be viewed with concern.

9. Self-control and temperance

Self-control is one of the cornerstones of reliability.

A person lacking self-control can be dangerous even when they seem friendly, intelligent, or well-intentioned. A lack of self-control weakens the ability to deal with:

- anger;
- sexual desire;
- money;
- food;
- alcohol;
- power;
- fame;
- frustration;
- jealousy;
- envy;
- fear;
- impulses of revenge;
- temptations of corruption.

9.1 Self-Domain Indicators

A person demonstrates self-control when:

- can hear "no" without retaliating;
- Respects boundaries;
- Don't try to force intimacy;
- It doesn't explode when contradicted;
- does not drive while intoxicated;
- does not use substances before critical responsibilities;
- it does not transform desire into right;
- Do not confuse freedom with irresponsibility;
- able to delay gratification;
- Corrections accepted;
- admits error;
- Repairs damage.

9.2 Indicators of lack of self-control

Warning signs:

- frequent compulsions;
- outbursts of anger;
- verbal aggression;
- possessive jealousy;
- sexual pressure;
- excessive alcohol consumption;
- reckless driving;
- vices;
- lies to hide habits;
- financial irresponsibility;
- inability to admit guilt;
- mockery of prudence;
- pride in being "dangerous," "crazy," "without limits," or "uncontrollable."

10. The relationship between health, discernment, and morality.

This report proposes that many social problems are connected to the loss of physical and mental health. A person who is malnourished, intoxicated, addicted, sleep-deprived, emotionally dysregulated, or mentally confused may have impaired judgment.

This does not mean that every social problem can be explained solely by health. Factors such as injustice, poverty, culture, impunity, education, family environment, trauma, and institutions also matter. However, health is an indispensable foundation for:

- to learn;
- to work;
- to love;
- to discern;
- resist manipulation;
- taking care of children;
- to serve society;
- to make responsible decisions;
- to preserve life.

Therefore, taking care of one's health should be considered a strong indicator of trustworthiness, especially when a person wishes to assume responsibility for the lives of others.

A criminal record can be a relevant indicator of trustworthiness, especially in high-risk contexts.

### 11.1 High alert crimes

Some backgrounds require extreme caution, especially in romantic relationships, childcare, spiritual leadership, teaching, working with vulnerable people, or public office. Examples:

- femicide;

- domestic violence;
- rape;
- pedophilia;
- sexual abuse;
- sexual exploitation;
- stalking;
- threat;
- kidnapping;
- human trafficking;
- torture;
- intentional homicide;
- serious fraud;
- corruption;
- extortion;
- mistreatment of vulnerable individuals.

In these cases, the history should not be treated as an irrelevant detail. It changes the level of risk.

11.2 Analysis Criteria

When evaluating a criminal record, the following should be considered:

1. seriousness of the act;
2. the relationship of the act to current liability;
3. recidivism;
4. elapsed time;
5. genuine repentance;
6. Repair of the damage;
7. Concrete life change;
8. Treatment performed, when necessary;
9. reliable testimonies;
10. Transparency about a person's past;
11. Risk for vulnerable people.

11.3 Prudent Application

The goal is not to declare that every person with a past mistake is beyond redemption. The goal is to protect lives.

When the risk involves women, children, the elderly, emotionally vulnerable people, public funds, professional management, or caregiving roles, the tolerance for risk should be lower.

12. Verifiable social reputation

The term "social status" can be ambiguous. It may seem that wealth, fame, beauty, popularity, or position make someone more trustworthy. This is not necessarily true.

The most appropriate concept is:

**Verifiable social reputation.**

Verifiable social reputation involves:

- history of good manners;
- absence of vandalism;
- respect for vulnerable people;
- testimony from mature people;
- consistency between public and private life;
- community contribution;
- family responsibility;
- job stability;
- absence of a history of violence;
- absence of practices involving public humiliation;
- good social interaction;
Recognition for useful service, not just for fame.

A poor person can have an excellent moral reputation. A rich person can be deeply dangerous. Therefore, a trustworthy reputation is not a reputation based on status, but a reputation based on character.

14. Alcohol, drugs and risk factors

Frequent alcohol consumption to the point of intoxication is a strong indicator of low reliability in roles that require attention, judgment, and responsibility for human lives.

Example: a person who frequently drinks cachaça to the point of drunkenness should not be given the responsibility of driving a truck, transporting passengers, caring for children, administering medication, operating dangerous machinery, or making critical decisions.

It's not just about moralizing. The relationship is functional:

**Intoxicating substance + loss of self-control + high-risk responsibility = threat to the lives of others.**

14.1 Applications

The warning is especially serious for:

- professional drivers;
- pilots;
- machine operators;
- doctors;
- nurses;
- caregivers;
- parents and guardians;
- teachers;
- police officers;
- security guards;
- religious leaders;
- risk managers;
- anyone who makes decisions involving the lives of others.

14.2 Heuristic

The greater the risk to the job, the less tolerance there should be for habits that compromise judgment.

15. Corruption as social self-sabotage

Corruption is a way of destroying collective trust.

A corrupt person seeks immediate advantage, but weakens the very social environment that sustains them. In an organic analogy, corruption behaves like a social pathology: it uses resources of the collective organism for private benefit while weakening the whole.

This analogy should be used as an ethical and social metaphor, not as authorization to dehumanize people. The point is that corruption:

- It destroys institutions;
- increases social costs;
- It harms the vulnerable;
- It encourages impunity;
- normalizes lying;
- It weakens justice;
- It destroys public trust;
- It transforms the common good into an opportunity for exploitation;
- sabotages the very society on which the corrupt individual depends.

Corruption, therefore, is a serious indicator of unreliability.

20. Test of the Seven Powers

A good heuristic for assessing confidence is to observe how the person handles different forms of power.

20.1 Power over information

Does the person keep it a secret or reveal it?

20.2 Power over money

Does the person provide accountability or embezzle funds?

20.3 Power over the vulnerable

Does the person protect or exploit?

20.4 Power in Conflict

Does the person engage in dialogue or humiliate?

20.5 Power in Anonymity

Does the person remain ethical or become corrupt?

20.6 Power when it errs

Does the person notice or manipulate?

20.7 Power when it is contradicted

Does the person respect boundaries or seek revenge?

The more a person uses power to preserve life, the greater the trustworthiness. The more they use power to manipulate, exploit, humiliate, destroy, or unjustly benefit, the lower the trustworthiness.

23. Summary of Heuristics

23.1 Preservation Heuristic

If a person values their own life and encourages others to value their own, they tend to be more trustworthy.

23.2 Self-Destruction Heuristic

If a person repeatedly destroys themselves, consciously and indifferently to the consequences, they tend not to be reliable for responsibilities involving other people.

23.3 Consumption Heuristics

What a person consumes, finances, recommends, and normalizes reveals part of their practical character.

23.4 Boundary Heuristic

Those who don't respect small boundaries probably won't respect big ones either.

23.5 Power Heuristic

Observe what a person does when they have power, anonymity, an advantage, or an opportunity to cause harm without being discovered.

23.6 Contrariety Heuristic

A person reveals a lot about their character when they hear "no".

23.7 Vulnerability Heuristic

Those who exploit vulnerable people should not be given deep trust.

23.8 Repair Heuristic

Making mistakes doesn't automatically destroy trustworthiness; refusing to repent, make amends, and make concrete changes does.

23.9 Pleasure Heuristic

When pleasure, money, power, or sex take precedence over life, dignity, and justice, trust must collapse.

23.10 Service Heuristics

Those who study, work, and undertake projects to serve society tend to be more trustworthy than those who seek only greed, status, dominance, or exploitation.

24. Difference between prudence and condemnation

Assessing trustworthiness is not the same as condemning a person.

Absolute moral condemnation declares that someone has no value. This report does not propose that.

Prudence asks:

Can I entrust my intimacy to this person?
Can I give money to this person?
Can I leave a child in this person's care?
Can I work with this person?
Can I marry this person?
Can I trust this person with sensitive information?
Can I allow this person to lead other people's lives?

A person can possess human dignity and, at the same time, be unreliable for a particular responsibility.

Example: an intoxicated person may still have human dignity, but should not drive a truck. A sexually compulsive person may still have human dignity, but should not receive deep marital trust without concrete change. A corrupt person may still be human, but should not manage public funds.

25. Guarding against injustice

For this metric to be fair, it is necessary to avoid serious errors.

25.1 Do not confuse involuntary illness with moral guilt.

Illness is not automatically a sign of a lack of character.

25.2 Do not confuse poverty with unreliability.

Poverty can be the result of social injustice, exploitation, lack of opportunities, or adverse circumstances.

25.3 Do not confuse status with virtue.

Wealth, fame, and position do not prove character.

25.4 Do not use rumors as evidence.

Reputation should be verifiable, not based on gossip.

25.5 Don't ignore real change

People can repent, make amends, and change. However, the change must be observable, consistent, and proportionate to the severity of the previous harm.

25.6 Do not disclose sensitive data unnecessarily.

Health, sexuality, family history, and criminal record should be treated with prudence, respect, and legitimate purpose.

26. Philosophical Synthesis

Trust should be considered part of an ethic of life.

A society remains alive when its members learn to:

- taking care of one's own health;
- to protect the vulnerable;
- study what is useful;
- to work in order to serve;
- Consume with discernment;
- Respect boundaries;
- to practice justice;
- reject corruption;
- to control impulses;
- Correct errors;
- to value the family;
- to preserve human dignity;
- Avoid vices, violence, frivolity, and exploitation;
- To develop self-love and brotherly love.

When a person is guided by these principles, they become more worthy of receiving information, power, intimacy, money, responsibility, and influence.

When a person is guided by self-destruction, lying, addiction, exploitation, violence, uncontrolled lust, corruption, futility, and disregard for health, they become less trustworthy, especially for responsibilities involving human lives.

27. Final Formulas

27.1 Reliability Formula

**Reliability = preservation of life + self-care + brotherly love + truth + justice + self-control + temperance + responsibility + useful service + reparation of errors.**

27.2 Unreliability Formula

**Unreliability = self-destruction + destruction of others + lies + addiction + violence + exploitation + uncontrolled lust + corruption + recklessness + futility + lack of remorse.**

27.3 The Trust Granting Formula

**The greater the responsibility, the greater the demand should be for evidence of health, temperance, honesty, reputation, self-control, and commitment to preserving life.**

28. Conclusion

The proposed indicators and heuristics for trust presented in this report stem from a legitimate concern: the survival of the human species and the protection of vulnerable people.

Trust should not be granted based on naiveté, neediness, appearances, romanticization, social pressure, or eloquent speeches. Trust should be granted when there is practical evidence that the person values life, respects boundaries, takes care of their health, controls impulses, rejects vices, practices justice, seeks useful knowledge, and works to serve society.

The strongest point of the proposal is the assertion that patterns of self-destruction and destruction of others are central indicators of untrustworthiness. This includes addictions, violence, corruption, sexual irresponsibility, recklessness, destructive futility, lying, and disregard for health.

Therefore, a life-oriented trust metric should always ask:

Does this person, through their habits, choices, consumption, relationships, history, and life plans, increase or decrease the likelihood of preserving their own life, the lives of others, and human society?

The answer to this question should guide the prudent granting of intimacy, power, influence, money, responsibility, and trust.
